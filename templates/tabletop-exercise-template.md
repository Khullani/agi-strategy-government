# Tabletop Exercise Template

Tabletop exercises were the highest-yield activity I used in the salon. They work because they force senior participants to argue from a seat that is not their own — which surfaces assumptions they did not know they were carrying.

Use them in Session 1, definitely. Use them in later sessions when the topic has a clear decision under uncertainty. Do not use them when the topic is conceptual or historical.

---

## The format that worked

Three escalating exercises in a single session:

1. **Strategic level** — a decision involving multiple national or international actors, with a 72-hour clock and irreversibility. Forces participants to reason about coordination failures, alliance dynamics, and the limits of unilateral action.
2. **Institutional level** — a decision inside one organization (a state, a city, a federal agency, a company), with competing internal mandates. Forces participants to reason about institutional incentives, political constraints, and the gap between what's optimal and what's possible.
3. **Tactical level** — an operational crisis with a tight clock, limited resources, and immediate consequences. Forces participants to reason about triage, communication, and what they would actually do at 6:14 AM on a Tuesday.

Each exercise is 12–15 minutes. Each participant plays a role outside their own seat.

---

## Structural elements per exercise

```
SCENARIO
[A specific, vivid setup. Concrete numbers. Real institutions. A time clock.
Two to three short paragraphs. No abstraction. The scenario is the prompt.]

THE QUESTION
[One sentence: what decision has to be made, by when, and what's at stake.]

ROLE ASSIGNMENTS

[Participant 1] plays [ROLE OUTSIDE THEIR SEAT]
Organization: [Specific institution]
Mandate: [What this role cares about — in two sentences]
The question for you: [Specific decision they have to defend, with constraints]

[Participant 2] plays [ROLE OUTSIDE THEIR SEAT]
... (same structure)

[Participant 3] plays [ROLE OUTSIDE THEIR SEAT]
... (same structure)

[Participant 4] plays [ROLE OUTSIDE THEIR SEAT]
... (same structure)
```

---

## What makes a good scenario

**Specific numbers.** "$4.2 million" not "a large ransom." "1,800 state employees affected" not "many workers." "72 hours" not "soon." Specificity is what makes participants commit to a position.

**Real institutions.** Use the actual names of real agencies, real bills, real companies. Drop the abstraction. If you're playing out a Colorado scenario, name the Colorado Digital Service. If you're playing out a federal scenario, name CISA. The realism is the curriculum.

**A clock.** Every scenario has a time pressure that forces a decision. "By morning." "Before payroll Friday." "In the next 72 hours." Without a clock, the scenario becomes a discussion.

**Asymmetric information.** Different roles know different things. The Lab CEO knows the technical capability. The National Security Advisor knows the intelligence assessment. The NATO Deputy Secretary General knows the allied positions. None of them has the full picture. The tension is the curriculum.

**Multiple defensible answers.** If there's a right answer, it's not a tabletop — it's a quiz. The scenarios that work best have no clean answer, only better and worse trades.

---

## What makes a good role assignment

**Outside the participant's seat.** The PE investor plays the AFSCME executive director. The chief of staff plays the Deputy National Security Advisor. The digital services technologist plays the EU Commissioner. The security scholar plays the federal agency advisor.

**A clear mandate.** Two sentences. What this role cares about, what they are accountable for. The mandate is the constraint that makes the role real.

**A specific decision under specific constraints.** Not "what would you do?" but "the board is split, delaying costs $400M, what do you do with the 72 hours?" The constraints force trade-offs.

**Permission to argue from the role.** State this explicitly at the top of the session: "You are not yourself. Play your role. The PE investor in the room is not the PE investor on this exercise — she is the AFSCME executive director, and she is going to argue like one."

## Worked example — Strategic level

Below is one of the three tabletops from my Session 1. Use it as a structural model.

```
🌐  STRATEGIC LEVEL — Exercise 1: The Model Drop

SCENARIO

It's Q3 2027. A U.S. frontier lab — backed by the largest PE fund in AI — is
72 hours from publicly releasing an open-weight model that represents a
genuine capability jump: it can autonomously conduct multi-step scientific
research, generate novel code exploits, and draft legislation
indistinguishable from human work.

U.S. intelligence believes Chinese researchers already have a copy of the
weights from an insider breach three weeks ago. The EU AI Act would classify
this model as "unacceptable risk" under its current framework. Turkey and
five other NATO allies are privately lobbying Washington for early access for
defense and law enforcement applications.

THE QUESTION

The release window is 72 hours. Every decision made now locks in the global
trajectory of this technology. Go.

ROLE ASSIGNMENTS

[PE participant] plays CEO of the AI Lab — Frontier Systems Inc.
Mandate: Shareholder value, research leadership, talent retention. You
believe open release is a competitive moat — it builds ecosystem, attracts
talent, and preempts regulation. Your board is split. Delaying the release
costs you $400M in projected enterprise contracts.
The question for you: What do you do with the 72 hours?

[IL Chief of Staff] plays U.S. Deputy National Security Advisor — The White House
Mandate: National security, allied relationships, innovation leadership.
China already has the weights. Blocking the release doesn't contain the
capability — it just means the U.S. loses the ecosystem advantage. But five
allied nations want controlled access, not open release. The President wants
a recommendation by morning.
The question for you: What do you advise?

[Turkish Scholar] plays NATO Deputy Secretary General for Emerging Security
Challenges — NATO HQ, Brussels
Mandate: Alliance cohesion, collective defense, interoperability. Five NATO
allies want early access for defense and law enforcement. The U.S. is
hesitating. If Washington blocks allied access while China already has it,
what does that do to the alliance?
The question for you: Do you push for a NATO-specific licensing framework,
or accept the open release?

[Colorado Digital Service] plays EU Commissioner for Digital Affairs —
European Commission, Brussels
Mandate: EU regulatory coherence, digital sovereignty, citizen protection.
The EU AI Act classifies this as unacceptable risk. If it's released openly,
it's immediately available to every actor in Europe — including those the
Act was designed to constrain.
The question for you: Do you threaten sanctions? Negotiate a carve-out? Or
accept that open-weight models make the Act unenforceable for frontier
capabilities?
```

## Notes on use

- **State the contract clearly.** "You are not yourself. Play your role." Repeat this if a participant starts answering as themselves mid-exercise.
- **Time-box hard.** 12–15 minutes per exercise, including debrief. Cut people off if they go long. The clock is the curriculum.
- **Debrief at the level above.** Don't ask "how did that go?" Ask "what assumption did you have to carry that you would never carry in your real seat?" The debrief is where the learning lands.
- **Build three exercises, not one.** Strategic, institutional, tactical. The escalation across scales is what makes the session memorable.
- **Use real, current events.** If there's a live municipal cyber incident in the news, build the tactical scenario around it. The realism is the curriculum.
