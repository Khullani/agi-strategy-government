# Reading List Format

Every reading in every session follows the same six-line structure. The format is the discipline — once you commit to it, you cannot include a reading you cannot defend.

---

## The format

```
Reading N (Label) — ~XX minutes
[Title] — [Source], [Date]
[URL]
[One paragraph of context — what this reading argues, why it's in this session, how it sits relative to the others.]
Discussion prompt: [One sentence. Anchored to a seat where possible. Action-oriented.]
```

---

## What each line does

**`Reading N (Label)`** — Numbered so participants can refer to them in discussion ("To Reading 2's point about scaling laws..."). Labels are short editorial tags — *Toolkit anchor*, *Philosophical anchor*, *Cautionary case*, *Optimistic frame*, *Empirical grounding*. They tell the participant how this reading is positioned in the set without making them guess.

**`~XX minutes`** — Actual estimated reading time, not aspirational. Be honest. Senior people respect honest time estimates and resent dishonest ones. If a paper is 12,000 words, say so.

**`Title — Source, Date`** — Source matters because senior people read by source-credibility first and topic second. Date matters because in this field a 2023 piece can be obsolete.

**`URL`** — The actual URL, on its own line, copyable. No "click here." No URL shorteners — they signal sloppiness and they break.

**`Context paragraph`** — Three to four sentences. What the reading argues, where it sits in the larger conversation, and most importantly *how it relates to the other readings in this session.* This is where you establish the tension. "Reading 1 makes the case for X. This piece makes the case for not-X. Read them together."

**`Discussion prompt`** — One sentence. Avoid open-ended reflection prompts ("What did you make of this?"). Use seat-anchored or decision-anchored prompts ("Where does this argument fail when you apply it to your jurisdiction?" or "What would the equivalent move in your seat cost, and who would oppose it?").

## Worked examples

### Optimistic frame

> Reading 1 (Optimistic frame) — ~25 minutes
> Preparing for Launch — Institute for Progress, January 2025
> https://ifp.org/preparing-for-launch/
> Argues that the trajectory of AI integration is path-dependent — early choices about deployment shape the long-run equilibrium. Three historical analogies (electricity, the internet, aviation) are used to argue that government is most consequential as an *active shaper* of the rollout, not as a downstream regulator. Read it as the affirmative case for fast, deliberate civic engagement.
> Discussion prompt: Which of the three historical analogies is closest to where you sit, and where does it break down?

### Cautionary case

> Reading 3 (Cautionary case) — ~25 minutes
> Clawed — Dean Ball, August 2024
> https://www.hyperdimensional.co/p/clawed
> A close reading of how SB1047 — the most-watched state AI bill of the year — collapsed despite serious bipartisan support. Read it as the dark side of Reading 2: the same toolkit, the opposite outcome.
> Discussion prompt: Same toolkit, opposite outcome. What constraint, if any, makes the difference?

### Empirical grounding

> Reading 2 (Empirical grounding) — ~20 minutes
> The 3.5% Rule and Civil Resistance — Erica Chenoweth, Harvard Kennedy School
> https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/why-civil-resistance-works
> Summarizes the dataset-driven finding that nonviolent campaigns succeed when they reach roughly 3.5% of population active participation. Note: Chenoweth herself has caveated declining post-2010 efficacy; include this caveat when discussing.
> Discussion prompt: AI safety is currently below 0.01% active participation. Is the 3.5% threshold the right yardstick, or is the analogy wrong?

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Including readings you haven't actually read.** Senior people can tell within five minutes.
- **Over-curating.** Three to four readings per session is the right number. Five is too many. Two is too few.
- **Reconciling tensions in your context paragraph.** Let the participants do the synthesis. Your job is to set up the collision.
- **Skipping the flagging of contested sources.** If a piece has a contested peer review history, methodological criticism, or a known bias, say so. The credibility you build is more than the credibility you lose.
- **Asking reflective discussion prompts.** "What did you make of this?" is the prompt of a study group. You're not running a study group.
