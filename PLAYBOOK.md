## Part I — The Thesis: Why Teach to Learn and Influence

You have just finished BlueDot. In the eight weeks behind you, you have read more about artificial general intelligence than most people working in government will read in the rest of their careers — the scaling laws debate, the contested distinction between alignment and control, the live policy fights in Washington and Brussels and Beijing, the resignation letters and the system cards and the analyses of both that proliferate on Substack — and you find yourself, in the days after, in that peculiar state familiar to anyone who has read seriously about a slow-moving emergency: an unsteady alloy of obligation and panic, neither of which has anywhere obvious to go.[^1]

The temptation, in this state, is to wait. To wait for one's institution to put out its statement, for one's principal to ask for a briefing, for the working group whose existence one keeps hearing rumored to finally extend an invitation. The difficulty with waiting, however, is that the senior person whose convocation one is awaiting is, with very few exceptions, waiting on someone of her own — for a green light, for a directive, for the deputy of the deputy to give the small nod that institutional life seems to require before any room can be built. The chain of permission, if one follows it patiently upward, terminates in an empty chair. The rooms one is waiting to be invited into have to be built by someone, and the people who turn out to build the rooms are very often the people who, somewhere along the way, simply got tired of waiting.

This playbook is about how one might become the person who has gotten tired.

Not, I should say at the outset, through the writing of another op-ed, the holding of another panel, or the joining of another working group that has been, by polite description, in formation since 2024. The proposal here is more particular: that one convene a small five-week salon, in one's own jurisdiction, for four to six senior practitioners who are working the same problem from four to six different angles and who, by some accident of the way our institutions sort people, have never been in the same room together. The larger argument of this book is that this is the most consequential single move available to a recent BlueDot alumnus in her first six months — more so, in my view, than almost any other thing she might plausibly do — and that the case for it rests on four observations, which I would like to take some time over.

The first is that one learns, by teaching, what one does not know. When I began my own salon I was reasonably certain that I was running a course about how to translate AGI strategy into terms a state government could act upon. By the fourth session my participants had quietly but unmistakably revealed to me that the people I had imagined I was teaching had already decided, among themselves, that legislation — the very lever I had structured my final session around — was the wrong primary instrument for the problem they were facing. I rebuilt that session over a weekend — new readings, new deck, new framework — and the rebuild has since become, by some distance, the strongest part of the curriculum. What is worth pausing over is that there was simply no way to have obtained that signal by reading. One obtains it only by gathering senior people in a room and listening, with a certain humility, to what they actually push back upon. The room, in this sense, conducts one's curriculum development on one's behalf. The price of admission is that one prepares the curriculum the room then sets about revising.

The second observation is that teaching builds standing in a register that no other activity readily builds. Senior practitioners, as a class, do not return telephone calls from people they do not know; they return calls from people who have already done something competent in their immediate orbit — which is to say, in the rooms through which they themselves circulate. A salon, on this view, is a competence demonstration that compounds rather than dissipates. The week after my own final session, three of four participants offered, without my having asked, to introduce me to people they thought ought to be in the next cohort. That sort of thing does not happen on the strength of a paper sent under cover of email.[^2]

The third is that the relationships, by the end, are the artifact. Five Wednesdays is a long time, and by the third of them one finds that the participants have begun to respond to one another rather than to the host. By the fifth they are introducing themselves to one another outside the salon, on their own initiative, for their own reasons. What one is left with, strictly speaking, is not the slide decks or the readings but a small, high-trust network of people who now have some plausible way to call one another about a hard problem when they need to — which, given the problem at hand, they almost certainly will.

The fourth, and to my mind the deepest, is that to teach is to influence by invitation rather than by argument. When one chooses the readings, and the framing, and who is seated next to whom, and the prompts that will open the discussion, one performs all of the labor of an op-ed without ever having to write one — and one performs it, importantly, in a setting in which the participants will leave under the persuasion that they themselves have arrived at the conclusion. That is, in my experience, considerably more powerful than any single piece of writing one is liable to produce in the same five weeks. It is, in fact, what op-ed writers tend to wish their op-eds did, and what only seldom they achieve.

The premise of the chapters that follow, then, is that a salon of this kind is the most consequential single thing one can do with one's first six months after BlueDot, and that it does its best work when it sits inside a larger portfolio of practices, of which I will lay out five others in Part II. Part III onward is the room itself: how it was built in one particular backyard, and how, with appropriate local translation, it might be built in yours.

[^1]: These appear to me to be the only honest responses available to a literate person at this juncture. Anything more composed ought, I think, to be examined for evidence of repression.
[^2]: The base rate on papers sent by email, in my admittedly small sample, runs somewhere between three and seven percent reply. The base rate on papers sent by email after the recipient has heard, through someone she trusts, that one has just run a five-week salon worth attending, is materially higher — not, I should say, because the recipient is doing one a favor, but because she has acquired, at no cost to herself, a fresh piece of evidence about whether one's paper is the kind worth opening.

## Part II — The Action Portfolio: Six Things to Do After BlueDot

Treat these as a portfolio, not a checklist. You will be better at some than others. You will get traction on some in year one and on others in year three. That is the point — they compound on different timescales and reinforce each other.

### 1. Write legislation

Not "write a paper about what legislation should look like." Actually draft the bill. Find a legislator's staff who is open to the conversation, learn their drafting conventions, and write the operative language. State legislatures are the most accessible — most state staffs are tiny and overworked, and a competent draft handed over for free is genuinely useful.

What this looks like in practice: identify one bill in your state on a topic you understand. Read it. Write a redline. Send it to the chief of staff with a one-page memo on what you changed and why. Repeat.

The standing you build with this is enormous and underrated. Legislators remember the people who showed up with text, not the people who showed up with concerns.

### 2. Testify

State committee hearings are the most underused testimony venue in the country. Sign-up procedures vary, but in most states a private citizen can submit written testimony on any bill in committee and request a slot for oral testimony. Most slots go unfilled. Your testimony will be read into the record, archived publicly, and cited.

What this looks like: track the bills in your state on AI, data, procurement, workforce, energy, and child safety. Pick one per quarter. Submit written testimony — three pages, technical, useful — and ask to be called. Eventually you'll be called. Eventually you'll be on the witness list as a matter of course.

This is also the first move that gets your name into press searches. Reporters scan committee witness lists.

### 3. Write op-eds

The op-ed is undervalued because most op-eds are bad. The good ones do one thing: they put a frame into a public conversation that wasn't there before. They are not about being right. They are about being upstream of a debate that is going to happen anyway.

What this looks like: write one op-ed per quarter. Pitch local first (your state's largest paper, your alma mater's alumni magazine, the trade press in your sector). Build a clip file. After four or five you can start pitching national. Keep them under 800 words. The discipline of compression is part of the value.

### 4. Write memos — and share them

The policy memo is the currency of governance. Two-page, well-cited, specific to a decision a specific person has to make. Most senior people would rather read a good two-page memo than a bad 40-page paper, and almost no one writes them well.

What this looks like: every time you finish a research thread you find interesting, ask yourself who, specifically, has a decision in the next ninety days that this would inform. Write the two pages. Send it to them. Cc nobody. Don't ask for a meeting. Just send.

A small fraction will read it. A smaller fraction will reply. The ones who reply become your network. The ones who don't reply will remember your name when they see it again somewhere else — testimony, op-ed, your course recruitment email. **None of these moves is wasted; they reinforce each other.**

### 5. Track data

This is the slowest-burn pillar and the most important. Pick something measurable about your local AI safety problem and track it consistently. Not because anyone is asking you to, but because in two years when someone needs the data you will be the only person who has it.

What this looks like, concretely: a spreadsheet of every AI-related bill introduced in your state, with sponsor, status, and a paragraph of analysis. A running tally of municipal RFPs that touch AI procurement. A log of every incident — outage, breach, deepfake, automated decision controversy — in your region. None of this is glamorous. All of it is leverage when the moment arrives.

The discipline of tracking changes how you read. You stop reading for opinions and start reading for data points. That is the skill BlueDot tried to teach you. Tracking makes you practice it weekly.

### 6. Teach to learn and to influence

This playbook is mostly about this pillar. But the reason it works is that the other five are running in parallel. Your course recruitment email lands harder because you have a clip file. Your readings list is sharper because you have been writing testimony. Your facilitator credibility holds because you have been tracking the data. **The salon is the keystone of the portfolio; it is not the portfolio.**

## Part III — The Salon: What I Actually Built

This section is the case study. Part IV is the how-to. Read this first so the how-to has shape.

### The course

**AGI Strategy for Leaders in Government: Navigating the Economic, Policy, and Security Impacts of Advanced Artificial General Intelligence.** Five sessions, Wednesdays, April 8 through May 6, 2026. Ninety minutes per session. Zoom. Chatham House Rules. Gallery view, not speaker view, the entire time.

Adapted from the BlueDot Impact AGI Strategy curriculum — meaning I started from their reading list and session arc, kept what worked for senior government professionals, and rewrote what didn't.

The arc:

| Session | Title | Frame |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Racing to a Better Future: The Civic Vision for AI Integration | What is the affirmative case for fast, broad AI integration in government, and what does it require? |
| 2 | Drivers of Progress: Scaling Laws & Economic Realities | How does the technical curve shape the economic reality? Why does compute buildout drive everything? |
| 3 | Mapping the Threat Landscape: Civic Vulnerabilities & Security | What are the actual failure modes municipalities and states will encounter first? |
| 4 | Defence in Depth: The Governance Toolkit (NIST, HB4705, and beyond) | What instruments actually exist — federal, state, voluntary, mandatory? |
| 5 | Beyond the Statute: The Six Levers of AI Governance | If legislation isn't the primary tool, what is? Procurement, standards, capital, adjacent law, choke points, civic & information. |

Note: Session 5 was originally titled *"The Action Plan: Moving from Theory to Legislation."* By the time we got to Session 4, the room had made it clear that the legislation framing was wrong for them. I rebuilt the entire session — deck, readings, facilitator guide — over the weekend before delivery. That rebuild is now the strongest part of the curriculum. More on this in Part V.

### The cohort

Four participants. Deliberately small. Deliberately heterogeneous.

- A **private equity professional** investing in AI-adjacent enterprise software
- A **state legislative chief of staff** in Illinois
- A **digital services technologist** at the Colorado Digital Service
- A **scholar of international security** at a Turkish institution

This composition was not random. Each one occupied a different lever of governance: capital, statute, procurement, geopolitics. Each one held a perspective the others did not have, and each one was senior enough to push back on the others without flinching.

If I had picked four people from the same field, the salon would have been a peer group. If I had picked novices, it would have been a training. Mixing seniors across fields is the move. The friction is the curriculum.

### The format

- **Zoom, gallery view, the whole time.** Speaker view kills cross-talk. Gallery view forces the room to read each other's faces. This is non-negotiable.
- **Chatham House Rules.** Stated at the top of every session. Reinforced when needed. Without this, senior people will not say what they actually think.
- **Cold-calling.** Not aggressive — direct. "Colorado, I want to come to you first. You're the only person in this room who has actually written a procurement that touched AI. What's the hardest thing about that?" Cold calls done well are an act of respect. They tell the room: I read your work, I know what you bring, and I expect you to bring it.
- **Parrhesia.** Greek term, candid truth-telling. I named it explicitly in Session 1. It signals to the room that the norm is not collegiality for its own sake but useful disagreement.
- **Deliberate collision of perspectives.** I designed reading lists to hold sources in tension rather than reconcile them. Reading 1 says X. Reading 2 says not-X. The discussion is the work.
- **No recording.** Tied to Chatham House. People speak differently when no recording exists.
- **Same time, same day, every week.** Senior professionals will not move their calendar. Wednesday at the same hour for five weeks. Period.

### The artifacts per session

Three deliverables. Every session, every week.

1. **A participant readings email**, sent the Thursday before. Plain prose, no sign-off. Each reading formatted as *Reading N (Label) — ~XX minutes*, with title, source, URL, one paragraph of context, and one discussion prompt.

2. **A slide deck**, used in session. Twenty to twenty-five slides. Branded. White background, one accent color, sans-serif body, serif display. Not a presentation in the corporate sense — a structure for the conversation.

3. **A facilitator guide**, internal to me. Run-of-show. Exact cold-call language. What to do if discussion stalls. What to do if discussion goes off the rails. What to do if someone hasn't done the readings. A page per major beat.

Session 1 also included **tabletop exercises** — role-assigned scenarios that escalated across strategic, institutional, and tactical levels, with each participant playing someone other than themselves. I'd do this again. The format works because it forces senior people to argue from a seat that isn't their own, which exposes assumptions they didn't know they were carrying.

## Part IV — The Operational Playbook: How to Run One Yourself

This is the how-to. If you've read Part III, you know what we're building toward. Here's the order of operations.

### Step 1 — Pick your salience

Don't pick "AI safety." That's everyone's topic and no one's responsibility. Pick the AI safety problem that is most salient in your specific backyard — the one where you have standing, where the conversation is already happening, and where the senior people you want in the room have already had to have an opinion.

For me in Chicago: state-level AI governance with active pending bills (HB4705 vs HB3773), a governor's data center pause, AFSCME negotiations on workforce impact, and federal preemption looming. I had four live policy fights to anchor a five-week arc.

What's yours? It might be:

- Municipal procurement and the first wave of AI-enabled vendors
- Healthcare AI deployment in your state's Medicaid system
- Schools, edtech, and the next generation of AI tutors
- Energy, data centers, and grid stress in your region
- Whatever is in your local press *and* in front of your local government *right now*

Pick one. The salience makes recruitment easy. Without it, you are running a study group.

### Step 2 — Identify your cohort

Four to six people. Heterogeneous across role, sector, and lever. Senior enough that they have opinions and standing in their own field, but not so senior that they will only show up if they can dominate. Look for people who:

- Already work on something adjacent to your salience
- Sit on a different lever than each other (capital, statute, procurement, operations, civic/media, academic)
- Will be in the same place for five Wednesdays
- Will tell each other the truth

You almost certainly already know two of them. The other two come through introductions. Spend three weeks on this. Recruitment is the single highest-leverage decision in the whole project.

A template for the recruitment outreach is in [`templates/recruitment-email.md`](./templates/recruitment-email.md).

### Step 3 — Adapt the curriculum

Don't write it from scratch. BlueDot has done the hard work; your job is to localize it.

For each session:

1. Take the BlueDot session's reading list as a starting point.
2. Replace one or two readings with a locally-grounded source — a bill, a memo, a piece of journalism, a state agency report. This is what turns the abstract into the specific.
3. Hold readings in tension. If reading 1 is the optimistic case, reading 2 should be the pessimistic case. Don't pre-reconcile. Let the room do it.
4. Write the discussion prompts as if you are writing for the specific people in the room. Mine were role-anchored — the PE seat got the capital prompt, Illinois got the statute prompt, Colorado got procurement, Turkey got geopolitics.

### Step 4 — Build the artifacts

For each session, in this order:

1. **Reading list and participant email**, sent 7 days before. (See the format in [`templates/reading-list-format.md`](./templates/reading-list-format.md) and [`templates/session-email.md`](./templates/session-email.md).)
2. **Slide deck.** Twenty to twenty-five slides. Each slide one idea. Use it as the conversation's spine, not as a presentation. (I used Node + pptxgenjs because I rebuild decks often and code is faster than clicking; whatever tool you use, version control your build.)
3. **Facilitator guide**, for you only. Cold-call language, time blocks, escape valves. (See [`templates/facilitator-guide-template.md`](./templates/facilitator-guide-template.md).)
4. **Tabletop exercises** for Session 1 specifically (see [`templates/tabletop-exercise-template.md`](./templates/tabletop-exercise-template.md)). Optional thereafter; some sessions are better as briefings or decision exercises.

### Step 5 — Run the salon

A typical 90-minute session:

- **0:00–0:05** — Welcome. Restate Chatham House. Restate that cold-calling is happening.
- **0:05–0:25** — My briefing on the session's frame. Twenty minutes, not thirty. Use the slides as a spine.
- **0:25–1:10** — Reading-driven discussion. I open with a cold call to the participant most in the readings' line of fire. Then I let it run, intervening only to redirect or escalate.
- **1:10–1:25** — Activity. Tabletop, decision matrix, role-played negotiation, or structured forecast. The activity is where the conversation lands.
- **1:25–1:30** — Close. Each participant gets one sentence. What's the move you'd make Monday on what we just discussed?

### Step 6 — Close the loop

The final session ends with two things:

1. **A wrap email** asking for anonymous feedback (use a Google Form or Typeform) and an attributed testimonial. The honest critique tunes the next cohort. The testimonial recruits the next cohort.
2. **An offer to stay in touch.** Most of the value of the salon accrues in the months after. Make it easy for participants to call you, and call them.

### Step 7 — Ship it publicly

When the cohort closes, write up what you learned. Publish it. This playbook is my version. Yours doesn't have to be a repository — a blog post, a Substack, a memo to your local AI policy circle, all fine. The point is that you compound the salon's leverage by making the next person's job easier.

## Part V — The Hard-Won Lessons

These are the things I did not know when I started, and the things I would tell you if we were having coffee instead of you reading a document.

### The framing your participants are skeptical of is the framing you should change

My Session 5 was originally about how to move from theory to legislation. By Session 4 it was obvious that the room thought legislation was the wrong frame — too slow, too captured, too easily preempted. I had two choices: ignore the signal and deliver the session I'd planned, or rebuild around what they were telling me.

I rebuilt. Forty-eight hours. New readings (CSET's *Governing AI with Existing Authorities*, Buterin's *d/acc: one year later*, Dean Ball's *Clawed*). New deck. New framework — six non-legislative levers and six methodological reframes. New role-anchored prompts.

It was the strongest session of the course. The lesson is: **the room is doing your curriculum development for you. Listen to it.** Your participants are senior practitioners. If they're skeptical of your frame, they're often right, and the rebuild is the actual deliverable.

### Distinguish bills with similar numbers

Illinois HB4705 (AI public safety and child protection transparency) is a completely different bill from HB3773 (AI in employment discrimination). They sound the same. They are not. Conflating them in front of a state legislative chief of staff is a credibility-ending error.

The general lesson: when your salience runs through policy, **track the specific bill numbers, dates, sponsors, committees, and current status,** and put them in your facilitator notes. Pre-bookmark the legislature's full-text URL for every bill you discuss. Refresh status before every session.

### Reading lists work best when they are in deliberate tension

For my historical-analogies reading list on collective action, the four readings were: Asilomar (insider self-regulation), the nuclear freeze movement (mass political pressure), Erica Chenoweth's civil resistance work (empirical research on what works), and the Montreal Protocol (international treaty with industry buy-in). Four readings, four theories of change, no reconciliation.

For my resilience-theories list: HRO/resilience engineering (operational), Roy Scranton on civilizational mortality (philosophical), Kyle Whyte on Indigenous ancestral dystopias (intergenerational), and Jem Bendell's Deep Adaptation (response framework). Four scales, four registers, no synthesis.

The discussion is the synthesis. Don't do it for them.

### Flag when sources are contested

For senior audiences, empirical grounding matters. If Bendell's *Deep Adaptation* paper has a contested peer review history, **say so in the readings email**. If Chenoweth herself has caveated the 3.5% threshold's declining efficacy post-2010, **say so**. The credibility you build by acknowledging the weaknesses of your own readings is more than the credibility you lose by including them. Participants notice.

### Role-specific prompts beat generic prompts

The single biggest improvement in my facilitator guides was when I stopped writing prompts like *"What do you make of this argument?"* and started writing prompts like *"Colorado, you've actually been the procurement counterparty in a contract that the EU AI Act would have classified as high-risk. What did the contract require that the Act doesn't?"*

The participant's seat is their leverage. Anchor every prompt to a specific seat.

### Assign roles outside participants' own seats in tabletops

For my Session 1 tabletops, I assigned each participant a role they did not normally occupy. The PE professional played the Frontier Lab CEO in the first exercise and the AFSCME executive director in the second. The IL chief of staff played the Deputy National Security Advisor. The Colorado technologist played an EU Commissioner.

This was deliberate. People reason differently when they are forced out of their default seat. They surface assumptions they didn't know they were carrying. The tabletops produced more usable insight than any of the briefings.

### The first cohort is an experiment. Tell people so.

In the recruitment email I called it "an experiment." In the welcome of Session 1 I called it "an experiment." In the wrap email I thanked them for being part of "my experiment." This is honest, and it is also strategic — it gives participants permission to push back on the format, and it gives me permission to change it on the fly.

The second cohort will not be an experiment. It will be a program. But the first one was, and naming it as such was the right move.

### What participants tell you when the course ends is the headline for cohort two

The feedback emails I received from participants after the course closed weren't just kind words. They were a precise diagnosis of what the format had done. One participant noted that the rare value was "the diversity of perspectives in the room and the openness of the discussions" and that AGI was discussable "not only as a technological issue, but also as a strategic, institutional, and governance challenge." Another noted "the intellectual honesty of the discussions and the willingness to challenge assumptions without losing the collaborative spirit."

Those are the marketing materials for cohort two. They are also the brief for what to preserve when I rebuild. **The participants will tell you what you built. Listen and write it down.**

## Part VI — The Long Game

I want to say one thing carefully, because it's the thing most people who start something like this get wrong.

**Almost none of what you do in the first year will get traction.**

You will write op-eds that get rejected. You will submit testimony to half-empty hearings. You will pitch a cohort and have three of five potential participants ghost you. You will publish a memo and hear nothing. You will run a course and one of the participants will quietly drop out after Session 2. You will check your analytics and the numbers will be small.

This is not failure. **This is the shape of the work.**

The actual mechanism is compounding. Year one you write four op-eds, three of which run somewhere small. Year two you write four more and one runs in a regional paper because the editor remembers your byline from year one. Year three you submit testimony on a bill and you are called to the witness table because the chief of staff who is now in a senior role was in your cohort in year two. Year four someone in another state runs a course on your model and cites your playbook. Year five you are in a room you couldn't have been invited to in year one, because of the cumulative weight of all the rooms you built yourself in years one through four.

The portfolio compounds. The salon compounds. The relationships compound. The clip file compounds. The data you track compounds. Almost nothing pays off on the first try. Almost everything pays off on the tenth.

**The discipline is to keep going when it doesn't feel like anything is happening.** This is harder than it sounds. The pull to stop — to wait for permission, to wait for traction, to wait for a clearer signal that the work matters — is constant. The way through is to make the work small enough that you can keep doing it weekly regardless of feedback. One memo a month. One testimony a quarter. One op-ed a quarter. One course a year. One spreadsheet, updated every Friday.

Set the cadence and protect it. The senior people you admire are not more brilliant than you. They are more consistent than you.

A few specific things I have found helpful:

- **Track your outputs publicly.** A page on your own website that lists everything you've published, testified on, taught, and tracked. Update it after each output. Looking at your own list is the antidote to the feeling that nothing is happening.

- **Find one peer who is doing the same thing in a different jurisdiction.** Trade drafts. Read each other's testimony. Read each other's op-eds. The peer is what keeps you accountable.

- **Refuse to scale prematurely.** The temptation after one good cohort is to run three the next quarter. Don't. Run one. Make it slightly better. The compound returns are in depth, not breadth.

- **Be generous with what you learn.** Publish your readings list. Open-source your facilitator guide. Send your memo to the person who needs it. The portfolio is non-rival. Sharing what you know does not diminish your standing — it builds it.

- **Show up for the work even when it is boring.** The Friday spreadsheet update is boring. The third op-ed that no one reads is boring. The grant application is boring. The senior people you want to be like did all of these boring things for a decade before anyone noticed.

You joined the BlueDot universe because you decided the AGI transition is a problem that requires more serious people doing more serious work. You are one of those serious people now. The question is not whether you are qualified — you are. The question is whether you will do the boring, weekly, unglamorous work for long enough that it compounds into something that matters.

That is the long game. Most people will not play it. The ones who do are the ones who will write the second draft of how this technology was governed.

I'd like you to be one of them. The room is waiting to be built.

— K.
